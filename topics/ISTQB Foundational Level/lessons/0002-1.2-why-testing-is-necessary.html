1.2 Why is Testing Necessary?

One exam favorite lives here: testing and QA are not the same thing, and the exam loves testing whether you know why.

FL-1.2.1 (K2) Exemplify why testing is necessary FL-1.2.2 (K1) Recall the relation between testing and quality assurance FL-1.2.3 (K2) Distinguish between root cause, error, defect, and failure

Testing's contributions to success (FL-1.2.1)

Testing is a form of quality control that helps achieve agreed test objectives within scope, time, quality, and budget constraints. The syllabus highlights three distinct contributions:

  • Detecting defects cost-effectively — removed by debugging (a non-testing activity), so testing indirectly improves quality.
  • Directly evaluating quality at various SDLC phases, feeding decisions like whether to release.
  • Indirect user representation — testers stand in for users' needs throughout development, cheaper than involving real users directly.

Testing may also be required to meet contractual, legal, or regulatory requirements. Crucially, this contribution isn't restricted to the test team — any stakeholder can use testing skills to bring a project closer to success.

Testing vs. Quality Assurance (FL-1.2.2)

People often use "testing" and "QA" interchangeably. The syllabus is explicit that they are not the same:

Testing

  • Product-oriented, corrective
  • A major form of quality control
  • Results used to fix defects

Quality Assurance (QA)

  • Process-oriented, preventive
  • Good process → good product
  • Results used as feedback on process performance
  • Responsibility of everyone on the project
Memory hook: testing is a form of quality control (one of several — others include formal methods, simulation, prototyping). QA is about the process that produces the product, not the product itself.

Errors, defects, failures, root causes (FL-1.2.3)

A chain, not synonyms:

Root cause
why the mistake happened
→
Error
a human mistake
→
Defect
fault/bug in the work product
→
Failure
if the defect is executed
  • Error (mistake) — made by humans, for reasons like time pressure, complexity, tiredness, or lack of training.
  • Defect (fault, bug) — what an error produces; can live in documentation, source code, or any work product. Undetected defects in earlier work products often lead to defective work products later in the SDLC.
  • Failure — what happens if a defect is executed and the system does something wrong. Not every defect causes a failure — some always do, some only under specific conditions, some never do.
  • Root cause — the fundamental reason a problem occurred (e.g. the situation that led to the error). Found via root cause analysis, usually done after a failure or defect is identified, to prevent recurrence.
Failures aren't only caused by errors and defects — environmental conditions (e.g. radiation affecting firmware) can cause them too.
